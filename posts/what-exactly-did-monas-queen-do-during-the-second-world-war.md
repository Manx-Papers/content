At Kallow Point in Port St Mary, a great black anchor stands overlooking the sea. Recovered from the seabed in 2010, it became the centrepiece of a memorial dedicated in 2012. Each year, people gather there to remember the Manx seafarers lost during the evacuation of 1940. The [Port St Mary RNLI's account of the annual service](https://rnli.org/news-and-media/2024/june/04/port-st-mary-rnli-attend-dunkirk-memorial-service) tells how the anchor came to rest on the Island.

It belonged to the *Mona’s Queen*. Most people who know her name know how she was lost. But there was much more to this ship's war than her last voyage.

## A wish that did not come true

The third *Mona’s Queen* was [launched at Cammell Laird in Birkenhead on 12 April 1934](/items/IME/IME-1934-04-13/0001/record/0). She was intended to carry holidaymakers across the Irish Sea. After the ceremony, Steam Packet director J. B. Waddington expressed a hope for the beautiful new vessel's future.

![A wish at the launch](section:IME/IME-1934-04-13/0001/block/R00005-B001?highlight="God+grant+she+may+never+be+required+for+any+war+service" "Waddington hoped the ship would never be needed for war service.")

Only five years later, war came. As the [National Army Museum explains](https://www.nam.ac.uk/explore/defeat-west), Britain and France were at war with Germany from September 1939 following the invasion of Poland. Britain began sending an army, known as the British Expeditionary Force (BEF), to France to fight alongside the French. Passenger steamers like *Mona’s Queen* were needed to carry men across the English Channel.

The [*Examiner* reported](/items/IME/IME-1940-06-07/0001/record/0) that *Mona’s Queen* was taken into wartime service almost immediately. Under Captain R. Duggan, she sailed from Southampton to Cherbourg with French reservists returning from Britain. Among them, the paper noted, were men who had once travelled around the Isle of Man selling onions.

![French reservists returning home](section:IME/IME-1940-06-07/0001/block/R00211-B000?highlight="Among+the+Frenchmen+on+board+were+several+who+had+tramped+the+streets+and+roads+of+the+Island.+sell-+ing+onions")
::: text
than 400 yards away from the Manx vessel.

Two days later, packed with French reservists who had been working in England, the Mona’s Queen was steaming out of Southampton bound for Cherbourg. Among the Frenchmen on board were several who had tramped the streets and roads of the Island, selling onions!

From that day up to the dramatic outbreak of the Blitzkrieg on May 10th, Captain Duggan’s ship steamed to and fro across the English Channel as a troopship, always exposed to the risks of mine and torpedo.

For months she crossed the Channel as a troopship, with mines and torpedoes an ever-present danger. On one journey, Duggan and First Officer R. Clucas counted **17 floating mines** in what was supposed to be a swept channel.

On 10 May 1940, [Germany launched its offensive against the Netherlands, Belgium, Luxembourg and France](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/dover-castle/history-and-stories/fall-of-france/). The speed of the advance transformed the work of the troopships: instead of simply ferrying troops across the Channel, they were increasingly needed to bring people back to safety. The *Examiner* said *Mona’s Queen* joined three other ships on particularly dangerous missions. Naval men had a name for the little group.

![Mines and missions of special danger](section:IME/IME-1940-06-07/0001/block/R00211-B001?highlight="17+floating+mines"+"Hell+Fire+Squad")
::: text
that on one day the Captain and his First Officer (Mr. R. Clucas) counted no less than 17 floating mines which had broken away in stormy weather and drifted into the swept channel.

From May 10th to the day she disappeared beneath the waters, the Mona’s Queen was engaged on missions of special danger with three other well-known ships. Before many days had passed, this select little fleet had won the title among all naval men in the command of the “Hell Fire Squad.”
:::

## Into burning ports

Her first dash during the offensive was towards **Rotterdam**, on a mission to evacuate Dutch troops. According to [Captain Duggan's account](/items/IME/IME-1940-06-07/0001/record/0), the city was in flames and burning oil had spread across the harbour waters. The ship escaped through thick smoke and was ordered to **Ostend**.

There, mines threatened the harbour entrance. A British destroyer eventually led *Mona’s Queen* in, and she took aboard refugees, shipwrecked sailors and Allied troops. Among the passengers was Mrs Callister, who had formerly lived in Douglas — proving that wherever a Manxman goes, he’s bound to meet someone from home.

Getting out was another problem. Duggan recalled being told the harbour held fourteen feet of water when his ship drew fifteen. A naval officer assured him there was soft mud beneath the keel. The steamer edged through it and got away.

![Refugees at Ostend](section:IME/IME-1940-06-07/0001/block/R00002-B002?highlight="if+there+wasn’t+a+Manx+person+among+the+refugees,+an+elderly+lady+named+Mrs.+Callister+who+used+to+live+in+Douglas" "The evacuation included a former Douglas resident.")

Next came the desperate struggle for **Boulogne and Calais**, two French ports on the Channel. As the Germans advanced, British forces were trying to hold the ports and prevent their facilities from falling into enemy hands intact. Newspaper accounts describe *Mona’s Queen* carrying a demolition party and a huge quantity of explosives to the French coast. Loading had taken fourteen hours; with everyone helping, the dangerous cargo was unloaded in three. The [*Isle of Man Times* placed this mission at Boulogne](/items/IMT/IMT-1940-06-08/0009/record/251), while the [*Examiner* named Calais](/items/IME/IME-1940-06-07/0001/record/0).

![Explosives for demolition](section:IMT/IMT-1940-06-08/0009/block/R00251-B002?highlight="300+tons+of+high+explosives" "The Isle of Man Times described the perilous cargo carried into Boulogne.")

At Boulogne, Duggan told the *Examiner*, the ship embarked troops as German forces closed in. Bombs fell on buildings near the quay, including the Imperial Hotel, from which British officers had escaped. *Mona’s Queen* got away with soldiers aboard as the town was being overrun.

And still her work was not finished.

## The last return

By late May, the German advance had cut off much of the British Expeditionary Force, along with French and Belgian troops, around Dunkirk, a port in northern France near the Belgian border. With their retreat blocked by land, the sea offered a way out.

The Royal Navy organised a vast rescue operation, codenamed Operation Dynamo, which ran from 26 May to 4 June. Destroyers, passenger steamers, fishing boats and smaller civilian craft worked together to bring the troops back to Britain. About 338,000 Allied servicemen were evacuated. [English Heritage’s account of Operation Dynamo](https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/dover-castle/history-and-stories/operation-dynamo-things-you-need-to-know/) explains the wider military situation.

Even before Operation Dynamo formally began, *Mona’s Queen* had been making rescue voyages. On an earlier voyage into **Dunkirk**, Duggan found the approach under heavy fire and the harbour difficult to navigate. He asked his crew whether they were willing to continue. They were. With sunken trawlers partly obstructing the entrance, the ship backed towards a battered quay and took aboard as many troops as she could carry. Clucas remembered soldiers advancing through the shelling, some supporting wounded comrades. [The ship brought them out](/items/IME/IME-1940-06-07/0001/record/0).

![The crew’s decision](section:IME/IME-1940-06-07/0001/block/R00214-B000?highlight="We+go+where+you+go,+captain" "The crew agreed to press on into Dunkirk.")

But on **29 May 1940**, *Mona’s Queen* was returning to Dunkirk under Captain A. Holkham, who had relieved Duggan for leave. As she approached the port, she struck a mine. Clucas, who survived, described the explosion that tore the vessel apart. She sank with the loss of **24 members of her crew**, including **17 from the Isle of Man**, according to the [RNLI’s account of the memorial](https://rnli.org/news-and-media/2024/june/04/port-st-mary-rnli-attend-dunkirk-memorial-service). [The *Examiner* printed the survivors' account](/items/IME/IME-1940-06-07/0001/record/0).

![Blown in two](section:IME/IME-1940-06-07/0003/block/R00084-B001?highlight="BLOWN+CLEAN+IN+TWO" "First Officer Clucas described the moments after the explosion.")

The loss was felt in homes across the Island. Weeks later, [Mrs Albert Murray spoke to the *Examiner*](/items/IME/IME-1940-06-28/0001/record/166) about her missing husband, a steward on board. Albert had already survived the sinking of the Steam Packet’s *Douglas* in the Mersey in 1923, when he had helped an unconscious stewardess to safety. During a recent visit home, he had reassured his wife about the safety of *Mona’s Queen*. Only after the sinking did she learn what risks the crew had been taking.

![A wife waits for news](section:IME/IME-1940-06-28/0001/block/R00166-B001?highlight="safe+as+a+house" "Albert Murray had tried to spare his wife the worry of knowing how dangerous his work was.")

There was one final tribute. In a [letter reported on 21 June](/items/IME/IME-1940-06-21/0005/record/235), Admiral Sir Bertram Ramsay, who had directed the evacuation, told Duggan what the ship had meant to the operation.

![The admiral’s tribute](section:IME/IME-1940-06-21/0005/block/R00235-B001?highlight="Mona’s+Queen+was+one+of+the+ships+that+we+could+always+rely+upon" "The admiral praised a ship whose crew had repeatedly answered the call.")

*Mona’s Queen* had spent almost the whole of the first nine months of the war carrying troops, before taking on the desperate work of rescuing soldiers and civilians as the German advance closed in. Dunkirk was the last chapter of an extraordinary war service, not the beginning of it.
