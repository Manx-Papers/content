In May 1939, two children at Ballabeg School were getting used to a world very different from the one they had left behind. Consuelo, aged nine, loved dancing. Seven-year-old Angelito liked arithmetic. Neither spoke much English, but both seemed to be settling happily into village life.

They were refugees from the Spanish Civil War, and the [Isle of Man Times](/items/IMT/IMT-1939-05-06/0007/record/64) reported that a Mrs Hall of The Level, Colby, had taken them in until their parents could make a home for them again.

![Spanish refugee children at Ballabeg School](section:IMT/IMT-1939-05-06/0007/block/R00064-B001?highlight=Consuelo+Angelito "Two young refugees find a home in the south of the Island.")

Four months earlier, a short story had appeared in *Mona's Herald* about a nurse tending the wounded in a makeshift hospital in Barcelona. The author's name was Manya Hall. Was there a connection between the writer and the woman who had welcomed the children to Colby?

The newspapers have a surprisingly rich story to tell.

## A small boat from Ireland

Two years earlier, in June 1937, a small motor vessel crossed the Irish Sea to Port Erin twice in the space of a week. Among its passengers were three young women. They were volunteers from the Youth Hostel Association of Northern Ireland, bringing the materials to equip the Island's first youth hostel at The Level, Colby.

The [Examiner reported](/items/IME/IME-1937-06-25/0010/record/112) that the house had bunks for about twenty people. Hikers and cyclists would do their own cooking and pay just a shilling for a night's accommodation. Temporarily in charge was an enthusiastic local member of the association: Mrs Hall.

![The Island's first youth hostel](section:IME/IME-1937-06-25/0010/block/R00112-B003)

![Mrs Hall takes charge](section:IME/IME-1937-06-25/0010/block/R00112-B001?highlight=Mrs.+Hall "The first report of the hostel names the woman in charge.")

In August the following year, Manya Hall wrote a lengthy [article about youth hostelling](/items/MNH/MNH-1938-08-02/0004/record/43) for *Mona's Herald*. She recalled the volunteers arriving from Ireland, soaked by the rain, with half-made furniture, and returning to help finish the job.

What interested her most, though, was not the accommodation. It was the people.

She invited her readers to imagine a gathering around a hostel fire: students from Liverpool, girls from Australia and New Zealand, a Norwegian doctor, a Belfast tobacco worker and a sixteen-year-old German boy with a dictionary tucked into the turn-up of his leather breeches. People of different ages, occupations and nationalities could meet on equal terms.

![Making friends across borders](section:MNH/MNH-1938-08-02/0004/block/R00043-B003?highlight=international+barriers)

Writing in 1938, with Europe moving towards war, she was particularly troubled by the Nazi regime's takeover of the German hostelling movement. Her own vision of hostelling was quite different: it was a means of bringing people together.

## The woman behind the pen name

A small item in the [Examiner's gossip column in September 1938](/items/IME/IME-1938-09-09/0008/record/260) provides the key to the mystery. Manya Hall was a pen name used by Mrs F. M. P. Hall of Colby.

![Manya Hall's identity revealed](section:IME/IME-1938-09-09/0008/block/R00260-B003?highlight="Manya+Hall")

Later [wartime reports](/items/IMT/IMT-1945-02-24/0006/record/197) give her first name and address: Florence M. P. Hall, of Homefield, Colby. In fact, her son Harry had given Homefield, Colby Level, as his address when he joined the [Examiner's children's league in 1935](/items/IME/IME-1935-06-14/0004/record/57).

The evidence points very strongly to Florence being both the hostel's first warden and the author of the article describing its origins. Whether Homefield itself was the hostel building remains to be established.

By March 1938, Florence was a [vice-president of the Manx Writers' Circle](/items/IME/IME-1938-03-04/0019/record/473). Through the group she met Eric Frank Russell, the Liverpool writer who was establishing a reputation for his science-fiction stories. Russell introduced her to the Swedish author Therese Ericson, with whom she announced plans to collaborate at a [meeting in May](/items/MNH/MNH-1938-05-24/0008/record/74).

![The Writers' Circle elects its officers](section:IME/IME-1938-03-04/0019/block/R00473-B000?highlight=vice-presidents)

Three of Florence's stories can be found in *Mona's Herald*. [Ill Bird](/items/MNH/MNH-1938-08-09/0002/record/11), published in August 1938, tells of an opportunistic criminal who plans to rob a village shopkeeper. [The Old Lady](/items/MNH/MNH-1938-10-11/0008/record/90), published in October, brings an elderly Manx shopkeeper to life with descriptions of her garden, her customers, the village gossip and its religious customs.

But her story published in January 1939 takes us much further afield.

## From Barcelona to Ballabeg

In [Red Harvest](/items/MNH/MNH-1939-01-03/0002/record/7), a schoolroom in Barcelona has been transformed into a hospital. Outside, the bombing has temporarily ceased. Inside, a nurse and a Basque orderly care for wounded patients, working together despite having no common language.

![A wartime hospital in Barcelona](section:MNH/MNH-1939-01-03/0002/block/R00007-B001?highlight=Barcelona)

The story is fiction, but Florence's interest in Spain was not confined to fiction. By May, Consuelo and Angelito were living under her care in Colby.

And there is another tiny but rather lovely detail in the archive. At [Laa Columb Killey in July 1939](/items/IME/IME-1939-07-07/0011/record/168), Angelito won second prize for weaving in his age group. The boy who had arrived speaking little English was now taking part in one of the parish's annual traditions.

What became of the two children is not yet clear. Nor do we know whether they were still living with Florence when, just a few weeks later, war broke out in Britain.

## Not her first war

Florence had already served in the First World War. In a [December 1938 article](/items/MNH/MNH-1938-12-13/0004/record/40) promoting the newly formed Auxiliary Territorial Service, she recalled her own time as an ambulance driver with the Women's Legion.

Seven women drivers had replaced ten men at a military hospital. Among them were a colonel's wife, a mill girl from Preston, a shop assistant and a young woman fresh from finishing school in Paris. They shared rations, cigarettes, night drives and the work of repairing the ambulances. On cold winter mornings, they took turns cranking the engines by hand.

For Florence, it was an example of why the suggestion that the new women's service was socially exclusive was nonsense. She knew from experience that women from very different backgrounds could work together.

![Florence remembers her ambulance-driving days](section:MNH/MNH-1938-12-13/0004/block/R00040-B002?highlight=ambulance+driver)

On 19 September 1939, Florence marched out of Douglas with the Manx ATS. The [list of women on active service](/items/IMT/IMT-1939-09-30/0010/record/229) published in the *Times* identifies her as a section leader and again gives her address as Homefield.

She continued to write for *Mona's Herald* from England. In [October 1939](/items/MNH/MNH-1939-10-24/0005/record/48), she described the women sleeping on straw mattresses, cooking bacon and eggs by torchlight in the rain, and singing *Ellan Vannin* on a darkened railway platform. By [February 1940](/items/MNH/MNH-1940-02-20/0008/record/86), they had endured a bitter winter and were facing separation from the Manx regiment to which they had been attached.

Her writing gives us a rare glimpse of the war through the eyes of an ordinary servicewoman. She acknowledged the hardships, but took enormous pride in what the women were doing.

## A family in uniform

In November 1939, the [Isle of Man Times printed a group photograph](/items/IMT/IMT-1939-11-11/0012/record/375) of Manx service personnel. Its caption identifies Mrs Hall with a cross and includes an inset portrait of her nineteen-year-old son from her first marriage, Nicholas Piskom, who was already a regular soldier.

![Manx service personnel and Nicholas Piskom](section:IMT/IMT-1939-11-11/0012/block/R00375-B000 "A rare opportunity to put a face to the name.")

By 1944, another [wartime report](/items/IME/IME-1944-06-23/0001/record/162) mentioned that Nicholas had a brother serving aboard HMS Belfast and a sister in the ATS. The following year, [news of Nicholas's serious wounds in Italy](/items/IMT/IMT-1945-02-24/0006/record/197) identified two of his brothers, Harry and Archie Hall, both serving at sea.

There was a particularly poignant detail. Archie was working aboard a Dutch hospital ship which sometimes called at Naples, where Nicholas was recovering. He [hoped he might be able to bring his injured brother home himself](/items/IME/IME-1945-03-30/0001/record/4).

Nicholas survived. The [Examiner reported in January 1948](/items/IME/IME-1948-01-23/0003/record/284) that he had been awarded the American Distinguished Service Cross, one of America's highest military decorations.

## A voice that would not be silenced

By May 1945, Florence was writing to the *Examiner* from Homefield. Her [letter on food rationing](/items/IME/IME-1945-05-11/0004/record/168) expressed her anger at what she believed was the better treatment of German prisoners and internees compared with Manx civilians. With her own children serving in the forces, she wanted the House of Keys to act and called for public meetings.

The letter is a reminder of how much the intervening years had changed Europe since her optimistic article on international friendship in 1938. The claims about comparative rations were her own, not independently established by the newspaper.

In November, [she complained about the Island's bus services](/items/IME/IME-1945-11-16/0005/record/168), asking whether passengers were suffering from the disadvantages of a monopoly.

And in January 1947, concerned about dogs being killed by motorists, she proposed a wonderfully drastic solution to speeding through villages: deliberately introduce potholes.

![A proposal to slow down motorists](section:IME/IME-1947-01-17/0005/block/R00234-B000?highlight=pot+holes)

The [Examiner's editor](/items/IME/IME-1947-01-17/0005/record/234) sympathised with her concern about speeding, but was distinctly less enthusiastic about the suggested road surfaces.

## Who was Manya Hall?

Florence Hall's story began to emerge from the newspapers with a passing mention of the woman in charge of a youth hostel. Behind that name was a writer, an ambulance driver, a servicewoman and a mother who gave two Spanish children a home when they needed one.

There is still a great deal we do not know about her. But thanks to the articles she wrote herself, we have something more revealing than a list of dates and achievements. We have her voice.
