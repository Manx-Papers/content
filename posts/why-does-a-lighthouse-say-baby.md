If you visit the Calf of Man today, look closely at the lower of Robert Stevenson's two old lighthouses. On the side facing Chicken Rock, you can just make out four enormous painted letters: **B A B Y**.

![Operation Baby](section:IMT/IMT-1954-12-04/0001/block/R00001-B000)

In [this photograph of the two lighthouses](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Two_disused_lighthouses%2C_Calf_of_Man_-_geograph.org.uk_-_737297.jpg), taken by Boakesey on 25 August 2006, you can just make out the faint white of the B beneath the window slot of the lower lighthouse. [CC BY-SA 2.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/2.0/).

In November 1954, Lieutenant-Commander Frank Williams had [been warden of the Calf for less than two months](/items/IME/IME-1954-11-26/0006/record/187). His 39-year-old wife was expecting their first child when a severe storm cut the couple off from the mainland.

As dawn broke on Tuesday 30 November, the keeper of Chicken Rock lighthouse spotted something through his binoculars. The word BABY had been painted on the disused lighthouse.

Almost simultaneously, a distress flare went up from the Calf. The keeper contacted the wireless station at Cregneash, and what the [*Isle of Man Times* dubbed “Operation Baby”](/items/IMT/IMT-1954-12-04/0001/record/1) was under way.

The man who started this dramatic story is a lighthouse keeper at Chicken Rock. As Tuesday dawned his ceaseless watching eyes were scanning the Calf.

As the light cleared he managed to make out writing on the disused Calf lighthouse. Through his binoculars he saw the word “baby.” Almost simultaneously a flare shot up from the Island. The keeper immediately got in touch with the wireless station at Cregneish and “Operation Baby” was in progress.
:::

The lighthouse keeper had received the message, but what could anyone do? The storm was so severe that even the lifeboats could not put to sea. The [*Isle of Man Examiner*'s description of the conditions](/items/IME/IME-1954-12-03/0004/record/148) gives a vivid sense of what the rescuers were up against.

::: text
ing ambulance.

The midwife, Nurse E. B. M. Heath, was also smiling as she joined her and two minutes later both were being whirled away to the Jane Crookall Maternity Home.

The “mercy mission” broke in the drab, dreary hours of dawn, eight hours before. The lighthouse keeper on Chickens Rock had sighted a pre-arranged signal from the Calf calling for immediate help. Distress flares were also fired as orders were sent out for the Port Erin and Port St. Mary lifeboats to stand-by.

But night-long, near-hurricane force winds had churned the coastal seas into a seething cauldron, ruling out even the remotest possibility of launching the boats.
:::

The answer came from an unexpected quarter. According to a [Ministry of Defence account of Royal Navy search and rescue](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/60-years-of-search-and-rescue), its first dedicated helicopter search and rescue squadrons had been established only the previous year.

Now, the RAF at Jurby was coordinating a rescue with the Fleet Air Arm in Northern Ireland. A Royal Navy Dragonfly helicopter flew from Eglinton to Ronaldsway, accompanied by an Avenger aircraft to maintain radio contact. It made one trip to the Calf with RAF doctor Squadron Leader French and another with Superintendent Nursing Officer Eleanor Heath, [bringing Mrs Williams back with her](/items/IME/IME-1954-12-03/0004/record/148).

At Ronaldsway, an ambulance was waiting to take her to the Jane Crookall Maternity Home. That evening, Nurse Heath described the rescue on BBC radio. Just six and a half hours after reaching the maternity home, [Mrs Williams gave birth to a little girl](/items/IMT/IMT-1954-12-04/0001/record/1).

![Mother Flown From Calf by Helicopter](section:IMT/IMT-1954-12-04/0001/block/R00001-B001)

The following day, [the helicopter pilot returned to the Calf](/items/IMT/IMT-1954-12-04/0001/record/1) to tell Commander Williams that he was a father.

The rescue even found its way into the [advertising columns of the *Green Final*](/items/GFL/GFL-1954-12-04/0002/record/52). The Victoria Tavern in Douglas, better known as the Dogs' Home, celebrated the happy outcome in verse.

![Good Work — the Dogs' Home advert](section:GFL/GFL-1954-12-04/0002/block/R00052-B000 "Even a Douglas pub celebrated the rescue.")
::: description
A Victoria Tavern advertisement headed Good Work, with a short celebratory poem praising the rescuers and wishing the baby a long life. Below the poem are the Dogs’ Home name, the slogan “Slip in — when you slip out!”, the Drumgold Street address and the name of proprietress E. McAleer.
:::

A week later, [*Mona's Herald* reported](/items/MNH/MNH-1954-12-07/0001/record/4) that the baby still hadn't been named.

The little girl spent her early childhood on the Calf. [A report in 1957](/items/TTS/TTS-1957-05-29/0032/record/203) described the island as having just three human inhabitants: the warden, his wife and their child. In September 1958, [the family moved to Kirk Michael](/items/RYC/RYC-1958-09-05/0008/record/104).

No telephone. No hope of a boat getting through. Yet four letters painted on a lighthouse wall had summoned one of the most advanced rescue machines of the day.
